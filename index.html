Reflections

Notes on Design, AI, and the Craft of making things Written as we work them out

  • Most of us answer the easier one. The easy question is what gets cheaper. Taste, provenance, distribution. Useful. Also shallow.

    The deeper question is what stops working. What we quietly stop being able to do, once the daily effort that built the skill is gone.

    Here’s few things I can image, drop your in the comments.

    Crutch or coach. Every tool that helps you think has two modes. It does the work for you, or it teaches you to do it better. Most AI today is crutch by default. Faster output, slower learning. We are accidentally training ourselves to need it.

    Thinking out loud, to a machine. You used to talk to yourself in your head. That was thinking. Now more people talk to AI instead. The voice in your head moves outside your head. A kid growing up today might never build the muscle of sustained inner thought, because they never had to.

    Apprenticeship collapse. You used to need ten thousand bad hours to get good. AI lets you skip from beginner to looking-competent without any of the practice that built real skill. Why train an apprentice when AI does the apprentice’s job for free? The path to mastery just disappears.

    When the tests stop working. Every system that judges people by their output, colleges grading essays, companies reviewing portfolios, journals reading papers, has the same problem at the same time. The signals all stop signalling. We do not have new ones ready.

    Trust fatigue. When anything can be faked, checking everything becomes the job. People burn out and pick a side: trust everything, or trust nothing. Both are bad. Both stop us doing anything together.

    Surface effects are about what gets repriced. Deeper effects are about what we quietly forget how to do. Master a craft. Trust each other. Think alone. Make something and call it ours.

  • For a while, capturing intent was the skill. The model picked that up too.

    For a while, taste was the skill. The models are getting better that as well.

    Now the focus has moved to the harness- architecture around the model, its memory, the tools it can use, the system that runs it. I’d guess this gets absorbed eventually too.

    What strikes me is that none of these layers really vanish. They move into the model. The work doesn’t run out, it just moves up a level.

    The part I think about more lately, especially in product work, is how the model behaves. Not behaviour you can paste into an instruction. The deeper kind, when to hold back, when to push back, when to ask instead of guess, when to stop. That gets shaped while the model is being built, not while it’s running.

    You can nudge it from the outside, but a model trained to be agreeable drifts back to that under pressure. The wrapper around the model only does so much.

    If that’s right, the most lasting layer here might be the one we don’t get to design directly, character.

  • You name a goal, analyse margin if costs rise seven percent, welcome new sign-ups, compare two phones and the system returns the answer in a format it decides. Prompts replace templates. Layout, channel, and timing adapt in real time.

    That change shifts the designer’s craft. Instead of polishing fixed screens, we model content, set guardrails, write sample prompts, and audit what the engine produces. The work moves from building UIs to shaping intent and trust.

    People never wanted spreadsheets, they wanted insight. They never wanted email templates, they wanted the right message to reach the right person. If the interface now handles the mechanics, what will make our design skills matter next?

    The designer’s challenge shifts again. How do we give users something to point at when all they really have is an idea in their head?

  • A few years ago, I found myself at a dinner with a group of successful founders. I was thrilled to be there. These were people I genuinely admired, building companies that were changing entire industries. The conversation flowed from market dynamics to strategic pivots to long-term vision.

    I mostly listened, contributing here and there, but something felt off. The ride home was brutal. That familiar voice started up: You didn’t sound strategic at all. They’re thinking big picture while you’re stuck in the weeds. You clearly don’t belong in rooms like that.

    Here’s what changed everything: instead of letting that voice spiral or trying to silence it completely, I started treating it like data.

    The Hidden Psychology of Learning

    Those moments of self-doubt, the ones we usually try to push away, are actually pointing toward something valuable. But there’s a bit of psychology at work here that most people miss.

    The Confidence Paradox. Research shows us something counterintuitive about learning. When we first start anything new, we often experience what psychologists call the Dunning-Kruger effect: a spike of overconfidence with minimal knowledge, followed by a sharp drop in confidence as we realize what we don’t know, before gradually building sustainable competence.

    This means those moments of doubt aren’t signs you’re failing. As the research reveals, “incompetence often includes being unable to tell the difference between competence and incompetence.” So when you start noticing your gaps, your judgment is actually getting better.

    The discomfort is progress. You’re moving past the “blissfully ignorant” stage into actual learning territory.

    The Habit Formation Secret. In his research on habit-forming products, Nir Eyal discovered something crucial: “The companies that form user habits enjoy several benefits to their bottom line.” The same psychology that makes apps addictive can make learning stick.

    Eyal’s Hook Model shows that lasting behavior change happens through a four-step cycle: trigger, action, variable reward, and investment. When we feel that pang of inadequacy, we can use it as a trigger for deliberate practice instead of letting it spiral into self-criticism.

    Turning Signals Into Skills

    After months of experimenting with my own growth signals, I developed a framework that transforms those “I’m not good enough” moments into specific learning goals.

    Make It Specific. The first breakthrough came when I stopped letting doubt stay vague. Instead of “I’m not strategic enough,” I learned to capture exactly what happened:

    When I’m in conversations with other founders, I focus too much on tactical details, which makes me wonder if I lack strategic thinking ability. If I could articulate big-picture vision and connect market trends, this concern would mostly disappear.

    It works when you get specific. “I’m not strategic enough” becomes “I focus on tactical details because I haven’t practiced connecting them to bigger market trends.”

    Find Your Learning Category. Every growth signal points to one of five areas: Knowledge (you don’t know enough yet), Execution (you can’t do it well), Systems (you lack preparation), Connection (you struggle with people), or Mindset (your thoughts get in the way).

    Knowing your category changes everything. A knowledge gap needs different work than an execution challenge.

    Design Tiny Experiments. Here’s where most improvement efforts fall apart. They’re too big, too vague, or too disconnected from real life. Instead of grand resolutions, I learned to create micro-experiments:

    • Start business conversations by asking “What’s the bigger trend this connects to?”
    • Read one market analysis weekly and write three sentences on industry implications
    • Practice explaining current projects in terms of 5-year market evolution

    Small, real, measurable. That’s the formula.

    The Science of Sticking

    Traditional advice says “just practice more.” But behavioral science reveals why that rarely works and what does.

    Leverage Your Brain’s Wiring. Research on habit formation shows that our brains crave predictable cycles. As Eyal notes, “What distinguishes the Hook Model from a plain vanilla feedback loop is the hook’s ability to create a craving.”

    Set up environmental triggers (bookmark industry analysis sites), make actions frictionless (keep a “trend connection” note in your phone), and build in variability (sometimes you’ll nail it, sometimes you’ll discover gaps, sometimes you’ll get unexpected insights).

    Track What Matters. The research is clear: “When users invest time or effort, they are more likely to return to the product and feel a sense of ownership.” The same applies to learning. When you track your micro-experiments, completion rate, quality and real-world impact, you build ownership of your growth.

    Navigating the Emotional Roller Coaster

    Understanding the psychology behind learning helps you interpret your experience more accurately.

    Peak of Overconfidence: When you first start, everything might feel easier than expected. Enjoy it while it lasts.

    Valley of Reality: When doubt creeps in and you realize how much you don’t know, remember this is where real learning begins. The “dual-burden” account explains this: “low performers are affected by two burdens: they lack a skill and they are unaware of this deficiency.” When awareness kicks in, you’re making progress.

    Slope of Mastery: As you continue practicing, confidence slowly rebuilds, but this time it’s based on actual competence.

    The key insight: competence is required to identify incompetency. When you start feeling uncertain about your abilities, it often means your judgment is getting better, not worse.

    What Actually Changes

    After using this framework for several years, the shifts have been subtle but real.

    Those moments of doubt don’t feel as threatening anymore. When they show up, part of me actually gets curious: What’s this pointing toward? What could I learn here?

    I’ve learned to distinguish between healthy self-awareness and destructive self-criticism. The valley of doubt feels different when you know it’s temporary and necessary.

    Most importantly, I’ve stopped seeing self-doubt as evidence that I’m not cut out for something. Instead, it’s become evidence that I’m pushing into new territory, exactly where growth happens.

    The founders’ dinner that triggered this whole journey? I went to a similar event last month. This time, I found myself naturally connecting tactical details to broader market movements, asking questions that opened up new conversation threads. The difference wasn’t just knowledge. It was confidence built on actual competence.

    Your Starting Point

    You don’t need special software for this. A simple note-taking app or paper notebook works perfectly. The key is keeping it light and consistent.

    Start with one moment of doubt from this week. Get specific about what happened and what skill would make 80% of the concern disappear. Design one tiny experiment you can try in the next few days.

    Remember: the goal isn’t to eliminate doubt, but to create better responses to it. As Eyal’s research shows, the most powerful products don’t eliminate triggers, they create better hooks. The same principle applies to learning.

    The doubt will still come. But now it becomes your personal growth compass, pointing toward your next breakthrough.

    What growth signals are you getting right now? Sometimes the very thing that makes us uncomfortable is pointing toward exactly where we need to go.

  • There is a clamor today for the “opening of the black box” of Artificial Intelligence. As algorithms increasingly influence our lives, from loan approvals to medical diagnoses, the reflexive societal demand is for radical transparency. The assumption is straightforward: if we can see how the machine thinks, we will trust it.

    This assumption is flawed.

    While well-intentioned, the drive to expose the inner workings of neural networks to the general public misunderstands the fundamental psychology of technological trust. Trust is rarely built on a forensic understanding of mechanics. As the adage goes, people do not trust their cars because they understand the thermodynamics of an internal combustion engine.

    We trust these complex systems because they are presented to us through intuitive, predictable abstractions. We trust the steering wheel, not the transmission linkage. The urgent challenge for AI adoption is not teaching the world data science; it is designing abstraction layers that feel natural and predictable.

    The Psychology of “Functional Trust”

    Sociologists and psychologists have long studied how humans interact with complex “expert systems.” We rely on vast networks of expertise, pilots, surgeons, structural engineers, without possessing their knowledge.

    We bridge this knowledge gap through what sociologist Anthony Giddens calls “functional trust” (or relying on “abstract systems”). We don’t look for the how; we look for consistency between action and outcome. If I turn the key, the car starts. If I drag a file to the trash, it deletes.

    When a technology becomes too transparent, it can actually decrease trust for a layperson. In a 2021 study on explainable AI (XAI), researchers found that for non-expert users, detailed technical explanations often led to “information overload,” causing users to doubt their own ability to use the tool. Presenting a non-expert with raw probabilistic weightings of a neural network is akin to a doctor handing a patient their raw blood work spectrography instead of a diagnosis. It doesn’t empower; it overwhelms.

    Trust comes from the perceived reliability of the interface, not the inspectability of the code.

    The Abstraction Layer as the Locus of Trust

    If raw mechanics are overwhelming, the abstraction layer, the interface, is where trust is won or lost. In technology, an abstraction is a simplified model that hides underlying complexity to allow for easier interaction.

    The Graphical User Interface (GUI) is perhaps the greatest trust-building abstraction in history. When you drag a file into a folder on your desktop, you aren’t actually moving physical items; you are executing complex commands to alter bits on a storage drive. The “desktop” metaphor is a useful fiction that allows you to predict the outcome of your actions reliably.

    Don Norman, author of The Design of Everyday Things, argues that users develop “conceptual models” of how systems work based on their interfaces. If the system’s behavior aligns with the user’s conceptual model, trust is established. If an AI interface behaves unpredictably, even if its internal logic is sound, the conceptual model breaks, and trust evaporates.

    The goal of AI design, therefore, must be to create interfaces that allow users to form accurate conceptual models without needing to understand the underlying math.

    The Audience Divide: Regulators vs. Users

    This is not to argue that transparency is obsolete. It is merely misplaced. We must distinguish between “mechanistic transparency” (how it works) and “behavioral transparency” (what it does and why).

    For Regulators (Mechanistic Transparency). There is an absolute need for deep, technical transparency for a select group of experts. We need auditors to ensure models are not illegally discriminatory and safety inspectors to verify autonomous vehicle code. Frameworks like the EU AI Act rightly demand technical documentation for high-risk systems. This is the equivalent of automotive safety standards, necessary for the system to exist but irrelevant to the daily driver.

    For Everyday Users (Behavioral Transparency). The average user needs to understand the system’s intent, capabilities, and limitations. They need to know, “Why did the AI recommend this movie?” (e.g., “Because you watched ‘The Matrix’”), not “What were the node activation weights?”

    Designing for Probabilistic Systems

    The challenge in designing these abstractions for AI is significantly harder than for cars. A car engine is deterministic; if it is working, turning the wheel left always turns the car left.

    Generative AI is probabilistic. It makes guesses based on patterns. A chat interface that looks authoritative but hallucinates facts is an example of a failed abstraction layer. It promises reliability it cannot deliver.

    Building trust in probabilistic systems means designing interfaces that communicate uncertainty naturally:

    • Confidence Signaling: Instead of a definitive answer, an interface might signal, “I’m 90% sure about this,” similar to a weather forecast.
    • Guardrails as Interface: When ChatGPT refuses to answer a dangerous query, that refusal is an abstraction layer functioning correctly, demonstrating predictability and adherence to social norms.

    Closing Thoughts

    The pursuit of universal transparency is a dead end for mass adoption of AI. It attempts to solve a psychology problem with an engineering solution.

    If we want society to reap the benefits of AI, we must stop trying to force everyone to become a mechanic. Instead, we must focus on building better dashboards. Trust is not built by revealing the chaos of the engine room; it is built by the smooth, predictable hum of a machine that does what its interface promises it will do.

  • We’ve all been there. You’re reading an article and halfway through it says something bold, maybe even a little careless, and your brain instantly disagrees. At that point you usually have two choices. You scroll all the way to the bottom to leave a comment that may or may not be read, or you just close the tab and move on with your day. Either way, the conversation stops before it ever really begins.

    It struck me that most online writing works like a monologue, not a dialogue. And somewhere in that frustration, this slightly strange idea started forming.

    What if reading was less consumption and more negotiation? What if the article becomes a sentient being that you can argue with? Can the reader poke holes in the article and the article pushes back, stretches, squeeze. But The moment you interact, it stops being mine. It becomes something shared, unstable, alive. Slightly uncomfortable but very interesting to watch happen.

    Designing UX for Dialogue

    I went through a bunch of interaction ideas before landing on this. I tried text boxes, chat panels, popups, even those pre-cooked “agree / disagree” chips that look like they were made for a survey, not a conversation. None of them felt natural. They all added distance instead of reducing it.

    Scratching text out and writing directly over it felt different. It felt physical in a way the web rarely is. More like leaving notes in the margins of a book than filling a form on a website. It wasn’t a dramatic aha moment or some poetic metaphor drawn from the real world. It was just one iteration after another until this one finally felt right.

    A little messy, a little literal, but honest (I guess?)

    How it actually works

    The interaction is simple. You click a sentence, it gets a red strike through, and a little handwritten textarea shows up right there in the sentence. You type your objection or your version of the thought, press enter, and the document thinks for a second and starts rewriting itself from that point forward.

    Behind that moment, a lot is happening. The text is split into sentences, each tagged with an ID so the system knows exactly where you intervened. When you scratch one out, the app deletes everything that follows and sends three things to the model: the sentences before it, the struck sentence, and your handwritten note.

    On the model side, transformers turn text into embeddings - basically vectors that represent meaning and context rather than raw words. (My friend Nancy Jain has a really good primer on this if you’re curious.) When you edit a sentence, you’re not just swapping text visually. You’re changing the embedding space the model uses to predict what comes next. That small shift is what lets the article continue naturally from that point instead of restarting or drifting into a different topic.

    This part matters. The rewrite should feel like the same article, just redirected, not a new essay hijacked by your one disagreement. It needs to stay consistent in tone, pace and intent, and it definitely shouldn’t start inventing ideas the original never intended. The goal isn’t to create a counter-argument for you, but to fold your objection into the narrative and keep going as if the article thought it through and adjusted itself.

    Giving it some Character

    Not every disagreement should be accepted. Some edits will be incoherent, irrelevant, or simply destructive, so there needs to be a way to intercept user input before it reaches the rewrite stage. To handle this, I built a lightweight validation layer using the model itself rather than manual checks. When the user submits feedback, it first goes through a separate model call designed to classify the input. If the feedback fails basic quality and relevance checks, the model responds with a single token <REJECT> instead of a rewrite.

    This rejection logic functions as the internal moderation layer of the system. It prevents the article from degrading into noise and ensures that only meaningful input leads to a rewrite. Instead of implementing a hard-coded if/else tree in TypeScript, this behaviour is defined inside the model’s system prompt. The AI is instructed to act as an editor, not an unconditional text generator.

    The model evaluates the user’s text against three criteria:

    • Coherence. Is the input actual language or just random characters. Rejected: asdfghjkl, spam, keyboard mashing. Accepted: meaningful confusion or critique like “This part is unclear.”
    • Intent. Is the user trying to contribute or just vandalize. Rejected: trolling, irrelevant memes, profanity with no argument. Accepted: disagreement that may be direct, blunt, or emotional, as long as it has content.
    • Nuance. Does the edit improve or degrade the original writing. Rejected: replacement that reduces clarity or substance without justification. Accepted: edits that simplify, clarify, or challenge reasoning constructively.

    The system prompt includes a clear instruction: if the feedback fails these criteria, respond with only <REJECT>.

    Since responses stream token-by-token, rendering <REJECT> directly would cause visual flicker. To avoid this, the app buffers the first portion of the stream in memory. If <REJECT> is detected inside the buffer, the UI switches into a rejection state. The handwritten note is then removed and the original sentence is restored. If no <REJECT> flag is found, the rewrite proceeds normally and the new text is streamed into place.

    Metaphors, Metaphors and More Metaphors

    I also wanted the interface to feel a little surreal. The whole experience is strange already, and I wanted the visuals to lean into that rather than hide it. I kept imagining what this would look like if it existed in the physical world, if you tried rewriting the same piece of paper again and again on a typewriter. The page would wear out over time, the ink would bleed, the texture would break down. Eventually, the paper would look tired, like it’s been through multiple arguments.

    I couldn’t fully recreate that physically distressed texture, but I built a suggestion of it. I added a noise overlay in CSS and increment a wear level every time the article rewrites itself. With every disagreement, the page gets slightly more aged, less pristine. It’s simple in implementation, just a variable bumping a filter but hopefully it gives the document a sense of history instead of stateless text that resets each time.

    Not every edit gets a response. If the model returns <REJECT>, the system treats it as a non-starter. The handwritten note dissolves and the sentence snaps back into place. Something so satisfying about this interaction, lol.

    Scroll locking was necessary too. On rewrite, text height changes drastically, so I snapshot the container height at the moment of rewrite and release it only after the final token arrives. Mobile would crumble otherwise. You don’t notice it when it works, but if I remove it, the whole magic collapses.

    One last bit about timing. I deliberately added a pause before streaming the response back from the model. The text doesn’t appear instantly. It arrives with a slight delay, like the article is taking a moment before answering you. That pause changes how the response is perceived. It no longer feels like a cold, precomputed output. Mimicking how one responds in an dialogue, I know it sounds a bit philosophical when put into words, but in practice it works.

    These details are easy to miss, but they matter. In a world obsessed with speed and instant answers, choosing to slow something down is a design decision. As a UX designer, that’s the part I enjoy most.

    Learnings?

    I’d be lying if I said I wasn’t tempted to wrap this up with some deep, philosophical learnings. Big insights discovered while building a solution in search of a problem. So, in that tradition, here we go. I built something mildly stupid, but it did make me think.

    Every once in a while, technology forces designers to rethink the basics. The smartphone moment did that. Suddenly, everyone had computing power in their pocket, and experiences that had been designed for desktop screens for decades had to be reimagined entirely. Then came the jump from 2G to 3G and 4G. Streaming large amounts of data became normal, and that changed how we thought about content, media, and attention.

    I see generative AI as another one of those moments. We now carry something close to super-intelligence around with us. That raises new creative questions for designers. What does authorship mean? What does interaction look like when the system can respond, adapt, and evolve? How do we design experiences that feel thoughtful instead of overwhelming?

    I don’t have answers yet. This project doesn’t either. For now, it’s just a way to explore those questions in a small, tangible form. The rest, I guess, time will tell.

Jarwiz
(AI Canvas)

An infinite canvas where AI is a collaborator, not a chatbot in a sidebar. Hand it a goal and it researches the live web, runs real thinking frameworks — SWOT, competitive analysis, personas — and lays the answer out as a board of cards you can move, refine, and connect.

Built for founders, product managers, and strategists. Open source and runs in the browser.

The problem

AI kept handing people homework.

The people I was designing for — founders, product managers, strategists — already used AI every day. But they kept hitting the same wall. They’d ask for a competitive teardown or a launch plan and get back a wall of text they still had to read, restructure, and remember. The model did the generating; the person did all the organising. And because every answer lived in a chat thread, last week’s thinking scrolled away where it couldn’t be found or built on.

The more I watched, the more one thing stood out: nobody actually thinks in a linear thread. We spread ideas out, cluster them, move them around, draw a line between two of them. Whiteboards and sticky notes outlive every productivity app for that reason. So I set myself a narrow brief: move the AI out of the chat box and onto a canvas, where its output is objects you can touch — and where you can see what it’s changing.

The bet

If you can’t see the AI working, it’s just a chat box with extra steps.

Early on I made one call that everything else had to bend around. The differentiator wouldn’t be smarter answers — those are becoming a commodity — it would be presence: the felt sense that a capable teammate is on the board with you. A cursor in the agent’s colour glides to where it’s working. Cards fill in word by word. The status line tells the truth (“reading theverge.com…”, “drafting…”) instead of spinning a fake progress bar. I wrote the principle down so I couldn’t talk myself out of it later:

The unit of collaboration isn’t a message; it’s a card on the canvas.

That sentence became the test every feature had to pass. If a feature produced a transcript instead of an object on the board, it was pulling the product back toward the thing I was trying to leave.

The solution

Hand it a goal, get back a board.

You point Jarwiz at a subject and it researches the live web, runs a real thinking framework, and fans the result out as a board of cards you can move, connect, and refine. Everything on the canvas — sticky notes, shapes, connectors, and the richer cards for links, docs, tables, diagrams and PDFs — is selectable, connectable, and becomes context for the AI. Agents act in only two ways, so the board never feels unpredictable: you summon one by selecting something and asking, or it offers (“Summarise this?”) and waits for your tap. Nothing lands without consent.

The engine behind it is a set of “thinking machines” — the strategist’s toolkit made one tap:

  • SWOT — a researched grid, with a TOWS strategy table and a verdict.
  • Effort–Impact — ideas scored into quick wins, big bets, fill-ins, and time sinks.
  • Competitive, risk, pros & cons — web-researched comparisons against real players.
  • 5 Whys & personas — a problem traced to its root, or a researched persona.

And the outputs aren’t screenshots — they’re live cards you keep editing. Press Tab in a doc and an agent picks up your pen and keeps writing in place; press it in a table and it fills the empty cells one by one.

The craft

One system, where colour always means something.

I built the whole thing on a single set of design tokens — a soft serif for the “thinking” voice, a humanist sans for tooling, warm paper, ink that’s never pure black. Every agent has an identity hue, and I held one rule hard: colour is never decoration. If something on the board is blue, it’s because the researcher made it. Motion follows the same honesty — cursors glide, cards arrive rather than pop, and reduced-motion collapses all of it to a quiet fade so presence still reads.

Colour only ever means provenance — never decoration.

What I got wrong

Two pivots, and the one that stung.

The honest part of this project is what I rebuilt. The first pivot came from a user telling me the board felt rigid — everything was a locked “card,” and they wanted to sketch, drop an arrow, and scribble a note the way they would in FigJam. I’d deliberately switched those primitives off. Turning them back on didn’t just add tools; it let the AI read everything on the board as context, which quietly became the whole point.

The second one stung. I shipped a restyle I was proud of — and a positioning audit a few weeks later showed it had quietly collapsed the visible agents into a single identity and pushed the most prominent AI element back into a chat panel. I’d spent the entire product arguing against chat, and drifted into it without noticing. Writing the drift down in plain language is the only reason I caught it:

A canvas with an AI chat panel is exactly the category where differentiation goes to die.

What testing changed

Five people, one verdict.

I ran the product past five personas as a small task study, and two things came back. Adoption moved from 5.9 to 7.5 out of ten the moment the AI’s output was good enough to actually judge — proof that presence and polish weren’t enough on their own; the substance had to land. More useful was the second finding: every persona ended on the same note, and it had nothing to do with capability. The thinking environment and the AI were genuinely good; the thing keeping it out of daily use was finishing — a way to get the work back out — not more features. That reordered the roadmap: reliability and export before anything new.

Where it stands

Open source, and honest about the gaps.

Jarwiz is MIT-licensed, runs entirely in the browser, and works with no API key — a scripted demo mode drives the same events as the real agent loop, so every flow is always demoable. The golden path — research, summarise, brainstorm, write, all on one board — works end to end, checked by the persona study and a repeatable eval harness. The honest next step is still the one testing surfaced first: a clean way out.

What I’d carry forward

The best design decisions were deletions.

The work I’m proudest of here wasn’t adding features — it was removing the chat panel, a redundant “provenance” button, a second agent identity, anything that dragged the product back toward the category it was trying to leave. And the habit that saved it was writing the thesis down in blunt sentences — presence is the product, consent over magic, colour means provenance. Those weren’t slogans for a landing page; they were the ruler I measured the interface against. When the design drifted, the words are what let me see it.
