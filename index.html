Reflections

Notes on Design, AI, and the Craft of making things Written as we work them out

  • There is a clamor today for the “opening of the black box” of artificial intelligence. As algorithms increasingly influence our lives — from loan approvals to medical diagnoses — the reflexive societal demand is for radical transparency. The assumption is straightforward: if we can see how the machine thinks, we will trust it. This assumption is flawed.

    While well-intentioned, the drive to expose the inner workings of neural networks to the general public misunderstands the fundamental psychology of technological trust. Trust is rarely built on a forensic understanding of mechanics. As the adage goes, people do not trust their cars because they understand the thermodynamics of an internal combustion engine. We trust these systems because they are presented through intuitive, predictable abstractions — the steering wheel, not the transmission linkage. The urgent challenge for AI adoption is not teaching the world data science; it is designing abstraction layers that feel natural and predictable.

    The psychology of “functional trust”

    Sociologists have long studied how humans interact with complex expert systems. We rely on vast networks of expertise — pilots, surgeons, structural engineers — without possessing their knowledge. We bridge that gap through what the sociologist Anthony Giddens calls “functional trust.” We don’t look for the how; we look for consistency between action and outcome. If I turn the key, the car starts. If I drag a file to the trash, it deletes.

    When a technology becomes too transparent, it can actually decrease trust for a layperson. In a 2021 study on explainable AI, researchers found that for non-expert users, detailed technical explanations often led to information overload, causing users to doubt their own ability to use the tool. Presenting a non-expert with the raw probabilistic weightings of a neural network is like a doctor handing a patient their raw blood-work spectrography instead of a diagnosis. It doesn’t empower; it overwhelms. Trust comes from the perceived reliability of the interface, not the inspectability of the code.

    The abstraction layer as the locus of trust

    If raw mechanics are overwhelming, the interface is where trust is won or lost. The graphical user interface is perhaps the greatest trust-building abstraction in history. When you drag a file into a folder, you aren’t moving physical items; you’re executing complex commands to alter bits on a drive. The “desktop” metaphor is a useful fiction that lets you predict the outcome of your actions reliably.

    Don Norman argues that users develop conceptual models of how systems work based on their interfaces. If a system’s behaviour aligns with the user’s model, trust is established. If an AI interface behaves unpredictably — even if its internal logic is sound — the model breaks, and trust evaporates. The goal of AI design must be to create interfaces that let users form accurate conceptual models without needing to understand the underlying math.

    Regulators vs. users

    This isn’t to argue that transparency is obsolete — it’s misplaced. We should distinguish mechanistic transparency (how it works) from behavioural transparency (what it does, and why). There is an absolute need for deep technical transparency for a select group: auditors ensuring models aren’t illegally discriminatory, inspectors verifying autonomous-vehicle code. This is the equivalent of automotive safety standards — necessary for the system to exist, but irrelevant to the daily driver.

    The average user needs to understand the system’s intent, capabilities, and limitations. They need to know “Why did the AI recommend this?” (“Because you watched The Matrix”) — not “What were the node activation weights?”

    Designing for probabilistic systems

    The challenge is harder than for cars. A car engine is deterministic; if it works, turning the wheel left always turns the car left. Generative AI is probabilistic — it makes guesses based on patterns. A chat interface that looks authoritative but hallucinates is a failed abstraction layer: it promises a reliability it cannot deliver.

    Building trust in probabilistic systems means designing interfaces that communicate uncertainty naturally. Confidence signalling: instead of a definitive answer, an interface might say “I’m 90% sure about this,” like a weather forecast. Guardrails as interface: when a model refuses a dangerous query, that refusal is an abstraction layer working correctly — demonstrating predictability and adherence to social norms.

    Closing thoughts

    The pursuit of universal transparency is a dead end for mass adoption. It tries to solve a psychology problem with an engineering solution. If we want society to reap the benefits of AI, we must stop trying to force everyone to become a mechanic. Instead, we must build better dashboards. Trust is not built by revealing the chaos of the engine room; it is built by the smooth, predictable hum of a machine that does what its interface promises.

  • We’ve all been there. You’re reading an article and halfway through it says something bold, maybe even a little careless, and your brain instantly disagrees. At that point you usually have two choices: scroll all the way to the bottom to leave a comment that may or may not be read, or close the tab and move on. Either way, the conversation stops before it ever really begins.

    It struck me that most online writing works like a monologue, not a dialogue. And somewhere in that frustration, a slightly strange idea started forming. What if reading was less consumption and more negotiation? What if the article became something you could argue with — where the reader pokes holes and the article pushes back, stretches, squeezes? The moment you interact, it stops being mine. It becomes something shared, unstable, alive. Slightly uncomfortable, but very interesting to watch happen.

    Designing UX for dialogue

    I went through a bunch of interaction ideas before landing on this — text boxes, chat panels, popups, even those pre-cooked “agree / disagree” chips that look like they were made for a survey, not a conversation. None felt natural; they all added distance instead of reducing it.

    Scratching text out and writing directly over it felt different. It felt physical in a way the web rarely is — more like leaving notes in the margins of a book than filling out a form. It wasn’t a dramatic aha moment, just one iteration after another until this one finally felt right. A little messy, a little literal, but honest.

    How it actually works

    You click a sentence, it gets a red strike-through, and a little handwritten textarea shows up right there. You type your objection, press enter, and the document thinks for a second and starts rewriting itself from that point forward.

    Behind that moment, a lot is happening. The text is split into sentences, each tagged with an ID so the system knows exactly where you intervened. When you scratch one out, the app deletes everything that follows and sends three things to the model: the sentences before it, the struck sentence, and your handwritten note.

    On the model side, transformers turn text into embeddings — vectors that represent meaning and context rather than raw words. When you edit a sentence, you’re not just swapping text; you’re changing the embedding space the model uses to predict what comes next. That small shift is what lets the article continue naturally from that point instead of restarting or drifting. The rewrite should feel like the same article, just redirected — not a new essay hijacked by your one disagreement. The goal isn’t to build a counter-argument for you, but to fold your objection into the narrative and keep going, as if the article thought it through and adjusted itself.

    Giving it some character

    Not every disagreement should be accepted. Some edits are incoherent, irrelevant, or simply destructive, so there needs to be a way to intercept input before it reaches the rewrite. I built a lightweight validation layer using the model itself: when you submit feedback, it first goes through a separate model call that classifies the input. If it fails basic quality and relevance checks, the model responds with a single token — <REJECT> — instead of a rewrite.

    Rather than a hard-coded if/else tree, this behaviour lives in the model’s system prompt: the AI is instructed to act as an editor, not an unconditional text generator. It weighs the input on three things — coherence (is it actual language, or keyboard mashing?), intent (contributing, or just vandalising?), and nuance (does the edit improve or degrade the writing?). Blunt, emotional disagreement is fine, as long as it has content; spam, trolling, and clarity-reducing edits are not. Since responses stream token by token, the app buffers the first part of the stream; if <REJECT> shows up, the handwritten note dissolves and the original sentence snaps back. Something so satisfying about that.

    Metaphors, metaphors and more metaphors

    I wanted the interface to feel a little surreal — to lean into the strangeness rather than hide it. I kept imagining what this would look like in the physical world: rewriting the same sheet of paper again and again on a typewriter. The page would wear out, the ink would bleed, the texture would break down until the paper looked tired, like it had been through multiple arguments.

    I couldn’t fully recreate that distressed texture, but I built a suggestion of it — a noise overlay in CSS, with a wear level that increments every time the article rewrites itself. With every disagreement the page gets slightly more aged, less pristine. It’s just a variable bumping a filter, but it gives the document a sense of history instead of stateless text that resets each time.

    One last bit about timing: I deliberately added a pause before streaming the response back. The text doesn’t appear instantly — it arrives with a slight delay, like the article is taking a moment before answering you. That pause changes how the response is perceived; it no longer feels like a cold, precomputed output. In a world obsessed with instant answers, choosing to slow something down is a design decision. As a UX designer, that’s the part I enjoy most.

    Learnings?

    Every once in a while, technology forces designers to rethink the basics. The smartphone did it — suddenly everyone had computing power in their pocket, and decades of desktop experiences had to be reimagined. I see generative AI as another one of those moments. We now carry something close to super-intelligence around with us, and that raises new questions: What does authorship mean? What does interaction look like when the system can respond, adapt, and evolve? How do we design experiences that feel thoughtful instead of overwhelming?

    I don’t have answers yet. This project doesn’t either. For now, it’s just a way to explore those questions in a small, tangible form. The rest, I guess, time will tell.

Jarwiz
(AI Canvas)

An infinite canvas where AI is a collaborator, not a chatbot in a sidebar. Hand it a goal and it researches the live web, runs real thinking frameworks — SWOT, competitive analysis, personas — and lays the answer out as a board of cards you can move, refine, and connect.

Built for founders, product managers, and strategists. Open source and runs in the browser.

The problem

AI kept handing people homework.

The people I was designing for — founders, product managers, strategists — already used AI every day. But they kept hitting the same wall. They’d ask for a competitive teardown or a launch plan and get back a wall of text they still had to read, restructure, and remember. The model did the generating; the person did all the organising. And because every answer lived in a chat thread, last week’s thinking scrolled away where it couldn’t be found or built on.

The more I watched, the more one thing stood out: nobody actually thinks in a linear thread. We spread ideas out, cluster them, move them around, draw a line between two of them. Whiteboards and sticky notes outlive every productivity app for that reason. So I set myself a narrow brief: move the AI out of the chat box and onto a canvas, where its output is objects you can touch — and where you can see what it’s changing.

The bet

If you can’t see the AI working, it’s just a chat box with extra steps.

Early on I made one call that everything else had to bend around. The differentiator wouldn’t be smarter answers — those are becoming a commodity — it would be presence: the felt sense that a capable teammate is on the board with you. A cursor in the agent’s colour glides to where it’s working. Cards fill in word by word. The status line tells the truth (“reading theverge.com…”, “drafting…”) instead of spinning a fake progress bar. I wrote the principle down so I couldn’t talk myself out of it later:

The unit of collaboration isn’t a message; it’s a card on the canvas.

That sentence became the test every feature had to pass. If a feature produced a transcript instead of an object on the board, it was pulling the product back toward the thing I was trying to leave.

The solution

Hand it a goal, get back a board.

You point Jarwiz at a subject and it researches the live web, runs a real thinking framework, and fans the result out as a board of cards you can move, connect, and refine. Everything on the canvas — sticky notes, shapes, connectors, and the richer cards for links, docs, tables, diagrams and PDFs — is selectable, connectable, and becomes context for the AI. Agents act in only two ways, so the board never feels unpredictable: you summon one by selecting something and asking, or it offers (“Summarise this?”) and waits for your tap. Nothing lands without consent.

The engine behind it is a set of “thinking machines” — the strategist’s toolkit made one tap:

  • SWOT — a researched grid, with a TOWS strategy table and a verdict.
  • Effort–Impact — ideas scored into quick wins, big bets, fill-ins, and time sinks.
  • Competitive, risk, pros & cons — web-researched comparisons against real players.
  • 5 Whys & personas — a problem traced to its root, or a researched persona.

And the outputs aren’t screenshots — they’re live cards you keep editing. Press Tab in a doc and an agent picks up your pen and keeps writing in place; press it in a table and it fills the empty cells one by one.

The craft

One system, where colour always means something.

I built the whole thing on a single set of design tokens — a soft serif for the “thinking” voice, a humanist sans for tooling, warm paper, ink that’s never pure black. Every agent has an identity hue, and I held one rule hard: colour is never decoration. If something on the board is blue, it’s because the researcher made it. Motion follows the same honesty — cursors glide, cards arrive rather than pop, and reduced-motion collapses all of it to a quiet fade so presence still reads.

Colour only ever means provenance — never decoration.

What I got wrong

Two pivots, and the one that stung.

The honest part of this project is what I rebuilt. The first pivot came from a user telling me the board felt rigid — everything was a locked “card,” and they wanted to sketch, drop an arrow, and scribble a note the way they would in FigJam. I’d deliberately switched those primitives off. Turning them back on didn’t just add tools; it let the AI read everything on the board as context, which quietly became the whole point.

The second one stung. I shipped a restyle I was proud of — and a positioning audit a few weeks later showed it had quietly collapsed the visible agents into a single identity and pushed the most prominent AI element back into a chat panel. I’d spent the entire product arguing against chat, and drifted into it without noticing. Writing the drift down in plain language is the only reason I caught it:

A canvas with an AI chat panel is exactly the category where differentiation goes to die.

What testing changed

Five people, one verdict.

I ran the product past five personas as a small task study, and two things came back. Adoption moved from 5.9 to 7.5 out of ten the moment the AI’s output was good enough to actually judge — proof that presence and polish weren’t enough on their own; the substance had to land. More useful was the second finding: every persona ended on the same note, and it had nothing to do with capability. The thinking environment and the AI were genuinely good; the thing keeping it out of daily use was finishing — a way to get the work back out — not more features. That reordered the roadmap: reliability and export before anything new.

Where it stands

Open source, and honest about the gaps.

Jarwiz is MIT-licensed, runs entirely in the browser, and works with no API key — a scripted demo mode drives the same events as the real agent loop, so every flow is always demoable. The golden path — research, summarise, brainstorm, write, all on one board — works end to end, checked by the persona study and a repeatable eval harness. The honest next step is still the one testing surfaced first: a clean way out.

What I’d carry forward

The best design decisions were deletions.

The work I’m proudest of here wasn’t adding features — it was removing the chat panel, a redundant “provenance” button, a second agent identity, anything that dragged the product back toward the category it was trying to leave. And the habit that saved it was writing the thesis down in blunt sentences — presence is the product, consent over magic, colour means provenance. Those weren’t slogans for a landing page; they were the ruler I measured the interface against. When the design drifted, the words are what let me see it.
